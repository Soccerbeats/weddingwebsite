## Brief History

Bali's history is nearly as colorful as its culture and landscape — constantly
evolving and adapting, yet never budging to the outside influences that war and
religious persecution constantly brought to the archipelago. From the rise of the
Majapahit Empire in the 13th century, which eradicated nearly every belief system
outside of Islam, to the later colonization by the Dutch, the Balinese held strong
to their unique blend of Hinduism and ancient animistic practices. Even still
today, with the large influx of outside influence growing by the year, the
Balinese hold strong to their high regard for cultural identity.

The Balinese practice a very unique form of Hinduism, only found here in
Indonesia — a country with a dominant Muslim population of nearly 90%. While it's
still relatively unclear where exactly these Hindu roots originate from, its
presence in the country can be dated back as far as the 1st Century. And as the
rise of Islamic rule in the country spread from island to island, eventually
pushing Javanese Hindu priests and artists away from their homeland, they were
able to find sanctuary in Bali — allowing for a cross-blend of animistic and
ancestral worship to blend cohesively with Hindu literature, ultimately giving us
the beautiful and unique neo-Hinduism found here today.

It's also important to note that while some names and practices do resemble that
of the more popular Hinduism practiced in India, one key difference is that the
Balinese are monotheistic, making it its own unique version of the religion.
Today, its originality can be seen prevalent across everyday life here, even in
the most touristic locales. Every business, home and public street is decorated in
daily offerings (called canang sari) as religious and ceremonial processions
parade through the streets, fully immersing you in this beautiful and truly unique
culture from the second you step off the plane at Ngurah Rai Airport. The
intricacies of the Balinese culture will welcomingly demand your attention, and if
you may be so lucky as to arrive during one of the countless vibrant holidays
celebrated here, then you'll be blessed with a unique show found nowhere else on
the planet, transporting you back in time, captivating and leaving you humbled by
the reverence these people hold for their traditions and community.

While its known history can be traced all the way back to the Stone Age, the
majority of Bali's rich legacy began during the rise of the Majapahit Empire in
the 13th century. While this period saw large numbers of Javanese Hindus migrating
to its neighboring island, they brought with them an abundance of art and
literature that laid the foundation for the culture we see so prominently here
today. After the fall of the Majapahit Empire in Java in the 15th century, a mass
exodus of Hindu priests, artists and royal families began setting roots alongside
the original Bali Aga (mountain Balinese) people.

By the 19th century, Dutch colonists had developed the Dutch East Indies, bringing
with it a new wave of integration that left European sailors in awe of this
mystical and resilient island, whom, through centuries of fighting off religious
persecution from their Javanese neighbors, stood firm in their beliefs and
ultimately gained its independent statehood by the 20th century.

## The People

If you ask anyone who's ever visited Bali what their biggest takeaway was, 99% of
the answers will begin with the people. The Balinese are known for being some of
the nicest, most generous and welcoming people groups in the world! Nowhere else
on the planet provides a sense of safety and security like Bali, for both men and
women travelers alike — and I would implore you to test this notion with an open
heart.

You can hop on a scooter and drive in any direction — to the highest mountain
point or the deepest jungle, until your bike breaks down — and within minutes,
you'll have an entire community of people there to help with a warm smile, a hot
meal and a bottle of local palm wine. But tread carefully, because while sweet in
taste, this stuff is STRONG! Believe me — I've personally experienced it more
times than I can count.

With a cultural foundation built on the principles of Karma and balance, a simple
smile will radiate warmth and hospitality in response. I know it may seem like a
bold or maybe cliche statement, but the energy that these people exude is
contagious — which is why so many people who come here find it so difficult to
leave. Once Bali has its hold on you, it's nearly impossible to pull away.

## Practical Information

**Language.** The official language in Indonesia is "Bahasa Indonesia", but most
people will refer to it as simply "Bahasa" or "Indonesian". However, when in Bali,
you'll also hear Balinese with a bit of Indonesian mixed in. What many people
don't know is that outside of being the largest archipelago in the world,
Indonesia as a country is actually home to the highest number of languages as well
(outside of Papua New Guinea) with 718 known languages currently and actively
spoken. In Bali alone, there are 3 different dialects of Balinese, outside of
Bahasa Indonesia and English.

**Currency.** Bali uses the Indonesian rupiah (IDR) as the local currency, which
roughly translates to 15,000 IDR to 1 US Dollar.

**Credit Cards and Banks.** While all major credit cards are becoming widely more
accepted, especially with the rise of foreign owned businesses, cash is still king
in Bali, so it's always safe practice to carry a handful of 10,000 to 100,000 IDR
notes for daily expenses and casual purchases. As of 2015, businesses are not
allowed to charge in dollars, so make sure you always have some rupiah on hand.
ATMs can be easily found all over the island, but always be sure to let your local
bank know of your travels ahead of time to avoid any alerts or holds on your
account.

**Climate.** In general, Bali enjoys warm weather year-round, but be mindful of
the rainy season from January to April and October to November if you're looking
to enjoy the wide array of outdoor activities the island has on offer.

**Water.** DO NOT drink tap water in Bali. Many tourists, travelers and expats
alike experience "Bali Belly" (severe stomach issues and indigestion) when
traveling to this region of the world, so it's best to avoid any unclean food or
water.

## How to Get Around

Bali may be a relatively small island (approx 5,780 km²) but there is A LOT to
see! If you plan to take full advantage of what this island has to offer, then
you'll need a way of getting around.

Now, while acknowledging that it may not be for everyone, there's no doubt that
the best and most convenient form of transportation here is motorbike, and
anything from 100cc mopeds to tour bikes, motocross and custom cafe racers can be
rented at a low cost, typically starting at as low as 50,000 IDR ($3.20 USD) per
day, and can be delivered directly to your hotel, villa or homestay upon arrival.
And with petrol prices being relatively low here, you'll never spend more than a
few dollars a day, even driving the extent of the island.

In Bali, motorbikes rule the roads, so while it may not be for the faint of heart,
it's by far the most convenient and enjoyable way to cut through traffic and see
everything the island has to offer. With this being said, you'll most likely need
to adjust your driving to that of the local pace. Road rules don't necessarily
exist here, but while it may feel like the most realistic game of Mario Kart
you've ever played, keep in mind that you only get 1 life before it's Game Over.
So while you may see countless visitors whipping around Canggu with no shirt, no
shoes and no helmet, PLEASE use your best judgment and protect yourself
accordingly. Not wearing a helmet is illegal in Bali, and if caught, you could
face a hefty fine that ranges in price depending on the mood of the policeman that
stops you.

Traffic is, and for the foreseeable future, will be an issue when traveling in
Bali. It's for this reason that I would suggest driving a motorbike over any other
3-4-wheeled vehicle. The roads were designed specifically for bikes, being
incredibly narrow and typically only providing 1 lane in each direction. To avoid
traffic when driving lengthy distances, it's good practice to time your longer
travels in the early mornings, or after 9pm. This will alleviate a lot of the
stress from your journey and believe me, your lungs will thank you! Sitting behind
trucks and cars that spit exhaust fumes in mid day heat certainly will not make
your "most memorables" list from your time here.

Another thing to keep in mind is that while traffic may be more hectic, the roads
in the southern portion of the island are some of the best in Indonesia. So when
first getting the hang of your new traffic weapon, it's wise to begin your trip in
this region before heading to the rougher terrain of the north.

One last rule-of-thumb for riding a motorbike in Bali — PLEASE DO NOT DRINK AND
DRIVE! And even if you yourself aren't intoxicated, tread lightly when driving
through crowded touristy areas such as Canggu, Uluwatu, Kuta or Seminyak at night,
as the most dangerous thing on the roads here are other drivers.

Now, if hopping on a motorbike and straight into the chaos of Bali traffic isn't
on your bucket list (seriously, be careful) then there are several other
convenient options available.

If you're not keen on driving a scooter yourself, then hop on the back of one! The
two most prominent apps you'll use when in Bali are Grab and Gojek, and should be
the first things you download upon arrival — think Uber, but far better and much
cheaper, costing less than $1 for most rides under 30 minutes! Both of these apps
not only include motorbike and car taxi services, but they also allow you to order
food, do your laundry, move house, fix your sink and just about any other service
you can think of. These guys are Bali's Heroes in Green, and they'll be your best
friend throughout your stay here.

If hopping on the back of a Gojek still doesn't seem like a comfortable fit, there
are also countless private drivers waiting to guide you through your stay on the
island. A private driver will cost roughly between $30 - $50 for a full day (8-10
hours), depending on the distance they'll be driving. It's even become a bit of a
running joke that anyone who's ever visited Bali has a good friend who is "the
best driver in Bali", whom they typically met outside of the airport upon first
arriving. And while it's a funny sentiment, it's actually true! Driving is a very
prominent and respectable career here, and local drivers take immense pride in
showing you their island! So if you fancy sitting back and gaining a private tour
guide out of your stay, then pick a driver and stick with them. Odds are, they'll
end up taking you back to their local village at some point to meet their family,
attend a local ceremony and enjoy local life through their own perspective. You
don't ever have to be shy with the locals here — they want so badly to show you
their island, and you'll end up making a lifelong friend out of it.

Bali does also have options available for renting cars, however, if you want my
honest opinion — it's just not worth it. Unless you are an incredibly skilled
driver with the patience of a buddhist monk, you'll have a miserable time, and
will most likely end up paying multiple fines for damages caused when you weren't
even in the vehicle. However, if you do insist on taking this option, then be
extra careful and prepare yourself to be honked at, flipped off and bumped into by
countless reckless other foreigners on bikes.

Now, at this point I'm sure you're thinking "well, it is an island… can't I just
walk?" Short answer, yes — depending on where you're staying and how far you
intend to travel. Some areas such as Kuta, Seminyak, Sanur, Canggu and Ubud are
very walkable, and even have sidewalks available, but it's still not preferable,
as motorbikes tend to use these sidewalks as their own private lanes during high
traffic times. While you certainly can, and most likely will be walking while in
town, the convenience of hopping on your own bike, or the back of a Gojek is so
much easier. Additionally, keep in mind that the deeper into the island you go,
there will be zero footpaths available outside of major towns and touristic areas.
Even Gojek and Grab are difficult to find once you begin straying out into the
more scenic and rural parts of the island.

## What to Do in Bali

Bali has something for absolutely everyone. With a rich and vibrant landscape
chock-full of untouched areas to explore, you'll never run out of things to do and
see here. From some of the best waves and surfing in the world, to volcanic
landscapes that peer out over the clouds, cliffside views, beautiful white and
black sand beaches, lush jungles, rolling rice fields, spectacular waterfalls, hot
springs and lava fields — the nature alone could keep you busy for the rest of
your life. Now let's throw in world class dining experiences, vibrant nightlife, a
deep-rooted art culture, exotic wildlife, beautifully unique and luxurious
accommodations and just about any outdoor activity you could imagine, and we're
just now beginning to scratch the surface of Bali.

## Ubud

It's a small, bustling town located in central Bali, making it one of the most
popular areas to base yourself with close proximity to all other regencies on the
island. Only 1.5 hours from Ngurah Rai International Airport, it sits perfectly
between the bustling areas to the south (like Canggu, Seminyak and Kuta) and the
stunning nature of the north — both of which are only an hours drive in their
respective directions. It's often regarded as a cultural and spiritual hub,
boasting stunning jungle views, preserved cultural traditions and an overall
feeling of calm — just outside of its lively city center (approximately 5km spread
in each direction from the famous arts market).

The known history of Ubud dates all the way back to the 8th century, where it
served as the island's center for natural medicine and spiritual healing. The word
*Ubad*, meaning medicine in ancient Balinese, is what gave it its original name.
Still today, people visiting from all over the world come here to experience a
wide-array of wellness, healing and spiritual retreats.

Ubud is, and has always been, one of the most sought after destinations to visit
in Bali, pre-dating the wave of Eat Pray Lovers in 2009. Its popularity really
started in the 1930's, when famous painters, writers and other artists became
mesmerized by the rolling rice fields, cascading waterfalls and rich cultural
traditions of the area. Over time, this is what gave Ubud its reputation as an
arts and crafts hub for the island.

While central Ubud has become increasingly more crowded over time (feeling like a
commercialized jungle theme park at first glance) what most people don't know is
that it's actually split into 14 separate villages — each of which hold their own
unique natural features and cultural traditions. This is really the main draw for
the wellness-inspired tourism that you see so prominently there today. Just a
stone's throw away from the bustling center of high class restaurants and shopping
boutiques, traditional village life carries on seemingly untouched, and within
close driving proximity to a plethora of the island's most iconic waterfalls.

**What you will do:** Yoga / Cacao Ceremony / Monkey Forest / Cooking and Ceramic
Classes / Waterfalls / "Cultural" experiences / Digital Nomads / Art / Healthy
Living / Unique nightlife / Sense of escape and natural immersion (depending where
you stay) / White Water Rafting / Jungle Buggys / Canyoning / Temples / Walking
around the Ubud Art Market

**Where you will stay:** With a wide range of accommodation experiences in Ubud
and its surrounding areas, it can be a bit intimidating to figure out which area is
best for you. From luxury 6 star resorts — set along pristine river valleys — to
private bamboo villas and humble homestays amongst the local village life, Ubud
really does have something for every type of traveler.

While placing yourself in any area of central or greater-Ubud offers diversity and
unique features of its own, it's important to keep in mind that any and all
attractions, activities and experiences are within relatively close proximity to
each other, and can be reached easily within a 10-30 minute drive. So choosing
accommodation in one area doesn't rule out any opportunities to experience all
that this city has to offer.

If it's your first time to Ubud, there's no better way to soak in the full blend
of "Ubudian" culture, history and lifestyle than a stay in the city center. Areas
surrounding the Sacred Monkey Forest and Traditional Art Market will plop you
right in the middle of the action, with the majority of restaurants, nightlife and
popular attractions within a short walking distance to all types of accommodation
(budget or luxury), making it the best option for travelers more keen to
foot-traffic than whipping around by scooter.

If you are looking to rent a bike or hire a driver during your stay, then there's
plenty of surrounding areas to choose from that will take you just outside the
chaos and bring you back to nature. While neighborhoods such as Penestanan and New
Kuning will provide a quieter experience amidst the stunning countryside, don't be
scared to stretch out a bit further to areas such as Tegalalang and Tampaksiring —
which set you right into some of the most beautiful nature and cultural
experiences that Bali has to offer.

**Where to party:** While Ubud has seemingly always held the reputation of "busy
by day, peaceful by night" — being known primarily for its yoga studios, rolling
rice fields, and Balinese Temples — it's also quickly becoming home to world class
cocktail bars and nightlife experiences, strategically set amongst lush jungle
views.

**Where to eat:** Ubud may be known primarily for being Bali's center for local
art and culture, but similar to Canggu and Uluwatu, it certainly holds its own
twist on beautiful and unique culinary experiences. From local warungs to Michelin
Star Restaurants, you'll have no problem finding amazing food for all budgets.
